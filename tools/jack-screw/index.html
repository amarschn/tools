How a power screw works

A power screw turns rotation into a straight push. Unwrap one turn of the thread and you get an inclined plane whose rise is the lead L and whose base is the circumference at the mean diameter, πdm. The angle of that plane is the lead angle:

$$\lambda = \arctan\!\left(\frac{L}{\pi d_m}\right) \tag{1}$$
  • L: lead, the axial travel per revolution (mm). Equal to the pitch on a single-start thread.
  • dm: mean (pitch) diameter of the thread (mm).
  • λ: lead angle, the slope of the unwrapped thread (deg).

Pushing a block up that plane against friction gives the raising torque. The thread flank is not vertical, though: on an Acme or trapezoidal thread it leans back by the half angle α, so the normal force on the flank is larger than the axial load and friction has more to bite on. That is the sec α term below, and it vanishes for a square thread.

$$T_R = \frac{F d_m}{2}\,\frac{L + \pi \mu d_m \sec\alpha}{\pi d_m - \mu L \sec\alpha} \tag{2}$$
$$T_L = \frac{F d_m}{2}\,\frac{\pi \mu d_m \sec\alpha - L}{\pi d_m + \mu L \sec\alpha} \tag{3}$$
  • TR, TL: torque to raise and to lower the load (N·m).
  • F: axial load on the screw (N).
  • μ: sliding friction coefficient on the thread flank.
  • α: thread half angle in the axial plane (0° square, 14.5° Acme, 15° trapezoidal, 7° buttress load flank).

Whatever carries the thrust adds its own drag. A plain collar rubbing on the frame contributes Tc = Fμcdc/2 in both directions, and on a hand jack it is often the larger half of the total. Swapping it for a thrust bearing is usually the cheapest torque you will ever buy.

Efficiency, and why it is so low

Efficiency is the lifting work divided by the input work over one turn:

$$e = \frac{F L}{2 \pi T_R} \tag{4}$$

Twenty percent is normal for a jack screw. That is not a defect. The friction that wastes the other eighty percent is the same friction that stops the load winding the screw back down, and a jack you can let go of is worth more than a jack that is cheap to turn.

Self-locking

A screw is self-locking when equation (3) comes out positive, meaning torque is needed to bring the load down. Rearranged, the condition is:

$$\mu \sec\alpha > \tan\lambda \tag{5}$$

Three things follow from that inequality, and each one has caught people out:

  • Lead, not pitch, is what matters. A four-start thread has four times the lead of a single start on the same pitch, and quadrupling tanλ is the fastest way to lose self-locking without changing anything you can see.
  • Collar friction does not count. This tool tests the thread alone. A screw that only holds because of collar drag drops its load the day someone greases the collar or fits a thrust bearing.
  • Static is not dynamic. Equation (5) is a static balance. Vibration lowers the effective friction coefficient, and a screw that holds on the bench can walk down under a running machine. Anything that must hold a load unattended gets a mechanical brake or pawl regardless of what this calculation says.

The checks that decide the size

Torque tells you what drives the screw. Four other numbers decide whether it survives.

CheckWhat it limitsWhat to change
Thread bearing pressureHow long the nut lasts before it wears into backlashLonger nut, larger diameter, softer duty cycle
Screw body stressYield at the minor diameter under load plus drive torqueLarger root diameter, stronger material
Column bucklingWhether a long screw stands up under compressionShorter free length, better end fixity, larger root
Whirl speedHow fast a driven screw can turn before it bowsShorter span, intermediate support, larger root

Bearing pressure spreads the load over the engaged threads, nt = Le/p:

$$\sigma_{bearing} = \frac{F}{\pi d_m f_d p\, n_t} \tag{6}$$

Threads do not actually share the load evenly. The mouth of the nut deflects least, so the first engaged thread takes roughly 38 percent of the total no matter how many are behind it. Past about ten engaged threads the extra length adds almost nothing, which is why a very long nut is not a fix for an overloaded one.

For buckling, which formula applies depends on how slender the column is. The transition sits at Cc = √(2π2E/Sy), where the Euler curve crosses half the yield stress:

$$P_{cr} = \frac{\pi^2 E A}{(L_e/k)^2} \quad (L_e/k > C_c) \qquad P_{cr} = A\left[S_y - \frac{1}{E}\left(\frac{S_y L_e/k}{2\pi}\right)^2\right] \quad (L_e/k \le C_c) \tag{7}$$
  • Le: effective length, the free length times the end-fixity factor (mm).
  • k: radius of gyration of the root section, dr/4 for a solid round (mm).
  • E, Sy: elastic modulus and yield strength of the screw (MPa).

Using Euler below the transition would claim a stocky column is stronger than the material it is made from, which is why the Johnson parabola exists.

Whirl is the first bending mode of the screw treated as a rotating beam. Run at it and the deflection grows until the nut binds:

$$\omega_n = (\beta L)^2 \sqrt{\frac{E I}{\rho A L^4}} \tag{8}$$

For a solid round, √(I/A) = dr/4, so whirl speed goes as root diameter over length squared. Doubling the stroke cuts the safe speed by four. Stay below 80 percent of the computed value: the derivation assumes a straight, uniform, balanced shaft, and yours is none of those.

When to reach for a ball screw

A ball screw swaps sliding contact for rolling, which takes efficiency from roughly 20 percent to roughly 90. The torque drops by the same ratio, and so does the heat the nut has to shed. The catch is in the reverse direction:

$$\eta_{back} = 2 - \frac{1}{\eta} \tag{9}$$

which is positive for any efficiency above 50 percent. A ball screw at 90 percent back-drives at 89 percent, so the load will run the screw backwards the instant the drive torque goes away. On an Acme jack a brake backs up a thread that already holds. On a ball screw it is the only thing holding.

Assumptions and limits

  • The thread half angle is taken in the axial plane. The exact treatment uses the normal plane, tanαn = tanα cosλ, which matters only for large lead angles. At the single-start lead angles typical of a jack the difference is under one percent.
  • Friction coefficients are starting (static) values. Running friction is lower, so the torque to keep a load moving is below the torque to break it away.
  • Bearing pressure limits are wear limits, not strength limits. Exceeding one does not break the nut; it wears the nut out.
  • Custom thread entry derives the minor diameter from proportional root clearance, which runs about 0.1 mm optimistic on fine pitches. Standard sizes from the dropdown carry their published minor diameters instead.
  • One load case at a time, applied statically. Shock loading, side loading and fatigue are not covered.
