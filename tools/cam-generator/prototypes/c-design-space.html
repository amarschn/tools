Reading the map

Bigger cams push more gently. The pressure angle φ is the angle between the force the cam applies (along the common normal) and the direction the follower can move. For an in-line translating roller, tan φ = (ds/dθ − e) / (√(Rp² − e²) + s), where Rp = Rb + Rr is the prime circle radius, e is the offset, s the lift and ds/dθ the lift per radian. Growing Rb grows the denominator, so φ falls.

Offset trades the rise against the return. Moving the follower axis sideways by e lowers φ on the stroke whose ds/dθ has the same sign as e and raises it on the other stroke. It pays off when the rise is faster than the return, or when only the loaded stroke matters.

Small cams also get sharp. The pitch curve's convex radius of curvature ρ shrinks with the cam. When ρ drops below the roller radius the cam surface folds over itself (undercut) and the follower cannot reproduce the motion. Designers keep ρ comfortably larger than the roller, often 1.5 to 3 times, to keep the Hertz contact stress down.

What this replaces. Textbooks size cams with nomograms that assume one motion law and a symmetric rise. This map is computed from your actual motion, so mixed laws, unequal rise and return angles and multiple strokes are all handled.
