What is a Manual J load calculation?

Manual J is the residential load calculation procedure from the Air Conditioning Contractors of America (ACCA). It estimates the heat a house loses in winter and gains in summer, for the whole house and each room, from its size, construction, windows, air leakage, ducts, and local climate. This tool does the same job using ASHRAE's published methods and physics, not ACCA's tables, so its numbers will differ somewhat from Manual J software.

What is Manual S, and how is it different from Manual J?

Manual J finds the load. Manual S picks equipment that meets it. A Manual S selection compares the load with the equipment's capacity at your actual design temperatures, then checks that it is neither too small nor oversized. The equipment step does this same kind of check for AC, heat pumps, and furnaces. Its oversizing limits are commonly cited values, not read from the current standard, so treat them as advisory.

Can this replace a Manual J report for a permit?

No. It is not ACCA-approved software, so it does not satisfy a permit that requires an approved Manual J report. Use it to understand your house, compare it with a contractor's report, or decide what to fix first. If you need a permit report, use approved software or hire a designer.

How many BTU per square foot do I need?

There is no single number, which is why the rule of thumb fails. For the same 2,000 ft² house above, cooling runs from 10 Btu/h per ft² in Seattle to 19 in Phoenix, and heating from 6 in Miami to 34 in Minneapolis. The old 500 ft² per ton rule would give every one of those houses 4 tons.

1. What this calculates

A load calculation answers one question: how much heat does this house lose on a cold design day, and how much must be removed on a hot one? Equipment should be chosen from that number, not from floor area or from the size of the old unit.

Oversized cooling equipment is the common failure. It reaches the thermostat setting before it has run long enough to pull moisture out of the air, so the house feels clammy, and it wears faster from cycling. ASHRAE warns about this directly in its residential chapter.

The results are design loads, in Btu/h. One ton of cooling is 12,000 Btu/h and one kilowatt is 3,412 Btu/h.

2. Manual J, S, T, and D

These are design manuals from the Air Conditioning Contractors of America (ACCA), and they run in order. The letters are not a sequence.

  • Manual J: the heating and cooling load of the house and each room.
  • Manual S: choosing equipment that meets those loads at your design conditions.
  • Manual T: choosing and placing registers and grilles.
  • Manual D: sizing the duct system.

This tool covers the first job and the core of the second. It answers the same question as Manual J from ASHRAE's published methods and first-principles physics. It does not use ACCA's tables, and its results will differ from Manual J software. Where a permit requires an ACCA-approved report, this page does not satisfy it. Use it to check a contractor's report, to understand your house, or to decide what to fix first.

Trademark note: Manual J, Manual S, Manual T, and Manual D are trademarks of ACCA. This tool is not affiliated with or approved by ACCA.

3. Heating equations

Heating uses the long-accepted worst case: the coldest design hour, with no credit for sun, people, or stored heat.

Equation (1): opaque surfaces and windows\( q = U A \Delta t \)
  • \(q\): heat flow, Btu/h
  • \(U\): U-factor, Btu/h·ft²·°F
  • \(A\): area, ft²
  • \(\Delta t\): indoor minus outdoor temperature, °F
Equation (2): slab edge\( q = F_p P \Delta t \)
  • \(F_p\): heat-loss coefficient per foot of edge, Btu/h·ft·°F
  • \(P\): exposed perimeter, ft
Equation (3): below-grade walls\( U_{avg} = \frac{2k}{\pi z} \ln\left(\frac{z + 2kR/\pi}{2kR/\pi}\right), \quad q = U_{avg} A (t_{in} - t_{gr}) \)
  • \(k\): soil conductivity, 0.8 Btu/h·ft·°F
  • \(z\): depth, ft
  • \(R\): wall and film resistance, ft²·h·°F/Btu
  • \(t_{gr}\): ground temperature, the annual mean less the seasonal amplitude, °F
Equation (4): air leakage\( A_L = 0.055\,\frac{ACH_{50} V}{60}, \quad IDF = \frac{I_0 + H \Delta t\,I_1}{1000}, \quad Q_i = A_L \, IDF \)
  • \(A_L\): effective leakage area, in²
  • \(V\): house volume, ft³
  • \(H\): stack height, ft
  • \(IDF\): infiltration driving force, cfm/in². \(I_0 = 698\) and \(I_1 = 0.81\) for heating at 15 mph, 343 and 0.88 for cooling at 7.5 mph
Equation (5): air load\( Q_{vi} = \max(Q_{unbal}, Q_i + 0.5 Q_{unbal}), \quad q = C_s (Q_{vi} + Q_{bal}(1-\varepsilon)) \Delta t \)
  • \(C_s\): 1.08 Btu/h·cfm·°F at sea level, reduced with altitude
  • \(Q_{unbal}\): exhaust-only or supply-only fan flow, cfm
  • \(Q_{bal}\): balanced fan flow, cfm
  • \(\varepsilon\): heat recovery effectiveness
Equation (6): ducts\( q_d = F_{dl} \sum q \)
  • \(F_{dl}\): duct loss factor from ASHRAE Chapter 17 Table 6, interpolated for insulation and leakage, then adjusted to the actual temperatures. Supply leaks do not depend on the surroundings, return leaks follow the room-to-surroundings difference, and conduction follows the supply-air-to-surroundings difference

4. Cooling equations

Cooling is harder because heat arrives late. Sun that hits a west wall at 3 pm reaches the room hours later, and a room can coast for a while on the heat its walls and furniture absorb. ASHRAE says plainly that methods with a fixed thermostat setting produce unrealistically high residential loads. This tool solves a full 24-hour clear design day instead of using a table.

Equation (7): outdoor temperature and sol-air temperature\( T_o(h) = T_d - x(h)\,DR, \quad T_{sa} = T_o + \alpha E_t / h_o \)
  • \(T_d\): design dry bulb, °F
  • \(x(h)\): ASHRAE's share of the daily range at each hour
  • \(DR\): daily range, °F
  • \(\alpha\): surface absorptance
  • \(E_t\): clear-sky irradiance on that surface, Btu/h·ft²
  • \(h_o\): outside surface conductance, Btu/h·ft²·°F
Equation (8): conduction through the real wall layers\( M_j = \begin{pmatrix} \cosh sL & \sinh(sL)/ks \\ ks\sinh sL & \cosh sL \end{pmatrix}, \quad s = \sqrt{i n \omega \rho c / k}, \quad Y_n = 1/\left(\prod_j M_j\right)_{12} \)
  • \(L, k, \rho, c\): thickness, conductivity, density, and specific heat of layer \(j\)
  • \(\omega\): daily angular frequency, \(2\pi/24\) per hour
  • \(Y_n\): the heat that reaches the room per degree of harmonic \(n\) of \(T_{sa}\)

This is the exact one-dimensional periodic solution, so brick and block get their real time lag. A test in the repository checks it against an independent finite-difference march.

Equation (9): windows\( q = A\left[U(T_o - T_i) + T_x\,IAC\,SHGC\left(E_D f_\theta (1 - F_{shd}) + 0.89 E_d\right)\right] \)
  • \(T_x\): insect screen transmission
  • \(IAC\): interior shade attenuation
  • \(E_D, E_d\): direct and diffuse irradiance
  • \(f_\theta\): loss of gain at steep sun angles
  • \(F_{shd}\): share of the window in the overhang's shadow that hour
Equation (10): attic heat balance\( T_{attic} = \frac{U_r T_{sa} + C_s Q_v T_o + U_c T_i}{U_r + C_s Q_v + U_c} \)
  • \(U_r\): roof deck coupling per ft² of ceiling
  • \(Q_v\): attic ventilation per ft² of ceiling, cfm
  • \(U_c\): ceiling U-factor
Equation (11): room air and thermal mass\( C_m \frac{d\theta_m}{dt} = G_{rad} - H(\theta_m - \theta_a), \quad 0 = G_{conv} + H(\theta_m - \theta_a) - UA\,\theta_a - Q \)
  • \(\theta_a, \theta_m\): air and mass temperature above the thermostat setting, °F
  • \(G_{rad}, G_{conv}\): radiant and convective shares of each gain, from ASHRAE Chapter 18 Table 14
  • \(C_m, H\): mass capacity and surface conductance
  • \(UA\): whole-house conductance
  • \(Q\): cooling delivered, capped at the capacity being tested

The solver finds the smallest steady capacity that keeps \(\theta_a\) within the allowed swing, 3 °F by default, which is the control assumption behind ASHRAE's residential method.

Equation (12): latent load\( q_l = C_l Q \Delta W + (68 + 0.07 A_{cf} + 41 N_{oc}) + q_{duct,l} \)
  • \(C_l\): 4,840 Btu/h·cfm at sea level
  • \(\Delta W\): outdoor minus indoor humidity ratio, lb/lb
  • \(A_{cf}\): conditioned floor area, ft²
  • \(N_{oc}\): occupants
  • \(q_{duct,l}\): humid air drawn in by return duct leaks

Each result line opens to show its equation with your numbers. Open Show the math under the drawing, or select any band on the section.

5. Where the weather comes from

Design conditions are computed from NOAA ISD-Lite hourly observations for 2011 through 2025, using ASHRAE's definitions: the 99% heating temperature is exceeded in 99% of the hours of the year, the 1% cooling temperature in 1% of them, and the wet bulb is the average during hours near that temperature. The data are public, and scripts/build_hvac_climate.py rebuilds every number. Because the record is recent, values can differ from older published tables. For Atlanta this method gives 91.9 °F, a 73.7 °F wet bulb, and a 16.5 °F daily range, against 91.5, 74, and 16.7 in ASHRAE's example, but its winter value is 28.9 °F against 26.5. Recent winters have been milder, so winter values usually run warmer than the numbers on older reports. Switch to the 99.6% basis under Change the design weather in the first step if that worries you.

ZIP codes map to the nearest station using US Census ZIP centroids. Elevation is the station's. In mountains or near large water, check that the station resembles your site.

6. Why the answer is a range

Each input you have not confirmed carries a plausible range. The tool reruns the whole calculation at both ends of each one, one at a time, and combines the effects as a root sum of squares. Entering a measured value narrows that input's range, so the band shrinks as you learn more. The ranking shows what to measure next.

The ranges are judgment, not statistics. They show which inputs matter, not a guaranteed confidence interval.

8. Limits

  • Not ACCA-approved. Do not submit it where a permit requires approved Manual J software.
  • Clear-sky July by default. Houses with large south-facing glass in mild climates can peak in fall. This version checks July sun only, so treat those houses with care.
  • Low-rise detached houses. ASHRAE warns that simple infiltration models do not apply to most apartments.
  • One thermal zone for the swing. Rooms share a thermostat here. Zoned systems need separate checks.
  • Equipment limits are advisory. ACCA sets a 90% lower limit and upper limits by compressor type. The upper values shown are commonly cited rather than read from the current Manual S, so check its Section N2 before relying on them.
  • Generic heat pump curves. Replace the starting point with your unit's published capacities.
  • Whole-house geometry is a rectangle until you enter rooms.
  • Your contractor still owns the design. A wrong input can be an expensive mistake, so review results with someone qualified before buying.
